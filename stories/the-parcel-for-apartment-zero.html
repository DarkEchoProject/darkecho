The Parcel for Apartment Zero

A courier accepts one final delivery to an apartment that does not exist. Inside the parcel is a way out, provided he can keep it unopened.

In this story

  1. The extra stop

  4. Outside

The extra stop

Owen's last delivery was not on the route when he loaded the van. It appeared after he finished his final scheduled stop: 18 Bell Lane, Apartment 0. One small parcel, no recipient name, signature required. The dispatch office had closed ten minutes earlier. The app offered him a late-delivery bonus large enough to cover the petrol.

He found the parcel beneath his empty crate. It was wrapped in plain brown paper, unusually careful at the corners, and weighed about as much as a book. Someone had written DO NOT OPEN IN THE BUILDING along the seam. Owen had seen stranger instructions. He photographed the label and drove to Bell Lane.

Number eighteen had six brass bells. They began at one. He rang the caretaker, who told him there was no apartment zero and that couriers always confused this building with the one around the corner. Owen checked the pin on the map. It pulsed directly beneath his feet.

While he was explaining, the intercom crackled. A second voice said, 'I'll take that.' The latch released. Through the glass door, Owen saw a narrow staircase leading down beside the main stairs. A light burned at its foot. He could have sworn the staircase had been a cupboard a moment earlier.

At the bottom was a gray door with a round brass plate. On it, freshly polished, was a zero. Owen knocked. Somewhere behind the door, a chair moved. Then a voice he had not heard for six years said, 'Leave it outside, mate. I'll get it in a minute.'

A familiar excuse

His brother used to say that through his bedroom door when he wanted to be left alone. Adam was not dead. He had moved abroad after a family argument and stopped answering calls. Owen had spent years composing messages that sounded casual enough to send. He never sent most of them.

'Adam?' The light at the top of the stairs went out. Owen looked back and found himself facing a second gray door, identical to the first. Where the main entrance should have been was another brass zero. His phone showed no signal. On the delivery screen, the recipient field had filled itself in: OWEN REED.

Behind the first door, his brother laughed. 'You took your time.' Owen moved the parcel away from the threshold. Something inside tapped once against the paper. He thought of a book sliding in its box, then felt a second tap directly beneath his thumb. The sound was too deliberate.

A woman spoke through the second door. 'Please don't answer him.' Owen turned. A piece of card slid under it. Printed on one side was an old delivery company's logo. On the other, in small neat writing: The parcel belongs outside. You are carrying the address in with you.

She said her name was Irene. She had delivered flowers here, years ago, to someone whose funeral she had attended. 'I opened the wrapping to see who sent them,' she said. 'You mustn't.' Owen asked how to get out. Irene went quiet long enough that he could hear her breathing through the keyhole. 'Find the place where you first knocked.'

The wrong doors

The corridor had acquired a turn. Owen followed it past four gray doors, each marked zero. Behind one, a television played the theme from a program his father used to watch. Behind another, someone was frying onions. The air was thick with the comforting smells of other people's dinners.

He took a photograph to mark the turn. In the picture, the corridor stretched straight ahead. There were no doors, only a concrete wall and the shadow of a staircase. A dark shape stood behind his shoulder. Owen lowered the phone and did not look around. The parcel grew heavier in his hands.

He found a fire-exit sign with an arrow pointing into the wall. Beneath it was a faded rectangle where another sign had been removed. A courier's initials were scratched into the paint: I.M. He knocked on the nearest door and asked whether Irene was still there. No answer. On the floor he found a dry black rose tied with delivery ribbon.

The first door had a scratch below its handle. Owen remembered making it with the corner of his scanner when he knocked. He went back, searching every handle. The voice behind each door became more insistent. His brother offered him a drink. His father asked him to stop making a fuss. Someone who sounded exactly like Owen begged him to put down the box.

At last he found the scratch. It was below a handle on a door that had no number. Owen touched the cut in the paint. Through the parcel came a rush of street sounds: a bus braking, rainwater in a gutter, someone wheeling a bin over paving stones. He held the package to his ear. The street was very close.

He nearly tore open the seam. Instead he remembered Irene's flowers. He put the parcel against the unnumbered door and listened. The street sounds moved through the wood. On the paper, his printed name faded. Beyond the door, Adam said, 'You can't leave me here again.'

Outside

Owen had no practiced answer to that. For six years he had imagined Adam admitting fault first. He stood on a nonexistent landing and finally said the less satisfying thing. 'I should have called you.' From the other side came a long, irritated sigh. It sounded like a person running out of lines to read.

He kept one hand flat against the parcel and turned the handle with the other. The door opened on Bell Lane. His van stood across the road with its hazard lights blinking. Owen pushed the parcel over the threshold before he stepped through. Inside the wrapping, something knocked so hard that a corner split.

He left it on the pavement and went back to hold the door open. 'Irene,' he called. A woman in an old blue delivery coat emerged from the dark. She had one shoe and a string of identification cards around her neck. Owen took her arm. As she crossed the threshold, the wrapping collapsed around a handful of dead flowers.

The caretaker found them beside a cupboard containing mops and a broken stepladder. Irene gave the police her name, her old address, and the name of her employer. She kept asking whether it was still October. Owen stayed with her until someone brought a blanket. His delivery app listed the final stop as canceled.

The bonus never arrived. Owen paid for his own petrol. Then he called the number he still had for Adam and left a message without rehearsing it. Three days later, while he was loading the van, his phone rang. His brother sounded older. The conversation was awkward, ordinary, and considerably harder than opening a door. Owen stayed on the line.
