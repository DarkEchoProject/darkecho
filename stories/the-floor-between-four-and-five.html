The Floor Between Four and Five

An elevator technician finds a landing that is missing from the building plans. Someone has been waiting there for the repair she promised to finish.

The complaint

The complaint said the elevator stopped between four and five whenever it rained. Everyone else in the maintenance office had crossed it off as a sensor fault. Leah kept the work order because of the second sentence: A woman on the landing says she knows the technician.

She reached Calder House at six, carrying a tool bag and the sandwich she had forgotten to eat. The caretaker showed her five buttons, five floors, and a recent inspection certificate. 'People hear the brakes,' he said. 'They make up the rest.' Then he asked whether she would be finished before dark.

On the fourth floor Leah found a fresh scrape inside the door track. She measured the leveling sensor, cleaned its bracket, and ran the car twice. Nothing unusual happened. Outside the stairwell window, rain began tapping the glass. The car made a third trip without her pressing a button.

It stopped where the floor indicator should have changed from four to five. Instead, the display went blank. The doors opened on a narrow landing with brown wallpaper and a pair of muddy children's boots drying on newspaper. At the far end, a woman in a green cardigan sat beside a radiator.

Leah recognized the cardigan before she recognized her mother. Her mother had been wearing it the afternoon Leah left for her apprenticeship, eleven years before she died. 'You've brought your tools,' the woman said. 'I thought you would.'

The loose handle

Leah wedged the doors with her bag. It was a bad habit she corrected in younger technicians, but her hands had begun working without instruction. She stepped onto the landing. Behind her, the motor hummed with a steady electrical note. Ahead, a saucepan rattled on a stove she could not see.

Her mother pointed at a door with a loose brass handle. 'You said you'd fix that before you went.' Leah had. She remembered the stripped screw turning uselessly in her fingers, her mother insisting that the taxi was waiting, the irritating relief of being allowed to leave something unfinished.

She took out a screwdriver. The screw head was soft with age, its slot almost gone. While she worked, her mother asked about her flat, her difficult shoulder, the job Leah had considered leaving. She knew ordinary things that had happened after her death. Leah kept looking up to find the trick in her face.

There was no trick. The woman had the small burn on her wrist from a baking tray, the crooked tooth she hid in photographs. When she smiled, Leah wanted to put down the screwdriver and lean her forehead against that familiar cardigan. Instead she asked where this landing went.

'Home,' her mother said. She nodded at the door. Warm light showed beneath it. Leah heard a chair scrape the kitchen floor, followed by her father's cough. He was alive in a care home thirty miles away. Here, his cough was younger.

A different work order

The replacement screw would not take. Leah emptied her tool bag to find a longer one, scattering cable ties and drill bits across the newspaper. Under the children's boots was a sheet of maintenance stationery. Her company's logo was at the top. Her own handwriting filled the description of work.

Return visit required. Do not complete repair. She had signed it with the shortened signature she used when she was tired. The date was tomorrow's. Beneath it, in another ink, someone had written: She always forgets why she left.

Leah turned toward the elevator. Her bag still held the doors apart, but the opening had narrowed. Through it she could see the real fifth-floor landing, its white paint and bicycle rack. A woman stood there holding a phone. Her hair was gray. She was staring straight at Leah, moving her mouth.

Leah leaned close enough to hear. 'Don't fix the handle,' the older woman said. She had Leah's old apprentice scar across her thumb. 'It only opens when you've finished. And once you've finished, there's always another thing that needs you.' Behind her, someone began knocking on a door Leah could not see.

Her mother sighed. 'You were always good at finding reasons to go.' She did not sound angry. That was worse. Leah looked down at the boots. They were her size when she was seven. The newspaper beneath them carried a photograph of a missing technician. In the photograph, she was wearing the jacket she had put on that morning.

Unfinished

The loose handle turned. On the other side, her father called her by the nickname no one at work knew. Leah put the longer screw back in its box. Then she removed the screw already in the door, placed it in her pocket, and said, 'I can't finish this today.'

Her mother stood. For a moment the radiator stopped hissing and the whole landing smelled of cold concrete. 'When will you come back?' she asked. Leah had spent years regretting every answer she had given too quickly. This time she took her time. 'I don't know.'

She lifted the tool bag out of the doorway. The older woman beyond the gap reached toward her, then stopped, as though remembering something. Leah stepped into the car. The doors shut on the cardigan, the boots, the small familiar kitchen sounds. On the display, five appeared.

The caretaker found her sitting on the elevator floor. He said she had been inside for ninety seconds. Leah asked for the building's original plans. A service void ran between the fourth and fifth floors, eighteen inches high. Nothing had ever been built there.

She drove to the care home instead of returning to the office. Her father was annoyed that she had missed dinner, then pleased with the sandwich she brought. She stayed while he told a story she had heard many times. When he fell asleep, she sat beside the bed, turning the stripped screw between her fingers.

The next morning her work order had disappeared from the system. In its place was a repeat call for Calder House, entered at 2:14 a.m. The fault description was three words long: Handle still loose. Leah marked the job unsafe, called her supervisor, and kept the screw.
