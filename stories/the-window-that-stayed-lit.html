The Window That Stayed Lit

Working nights, a translator begins exchanging notes with a neighbor across the courtyard. Then the neighbor asks why his room has no door.

Another night worker

For the first month in the flat, Daniel's only neighbor was a square of yellow light across the courtyard. Other windows darkened after midnight. That one stayed lit until dawn. Behind it an older woman sat at a table, her hands moving over something too small for Daniel to see.

He translated technical manuals at night because his clients were eight time zones away. The courtyard was narrow enough to hear a teaspoon dropped in another kitchen, yet neither of them made much noise. Sometimes the woman looked up when he did. Eventually they began raising their cups to each other.

One Thursday she held a sheet of paper against the glass. TEA OR COFFEE? Daniel wrote COFFEE on the back of a printer invoice. She shook her head and held up a second page: THAT EXPLAINS THE HOURS. He laughed louder than he had meant to. It startled him to realize she could probably hear it.

Her name was Ruth. She repaired old books for a charity shop, working through the night when her hands would not let her sleep. Daniel told her about a manual that contained eleven different names for the same valve. Ruth wrote: SOME THINGS NEED MORE THAN ONE NAME. Then, beneath it: MY SON CALLS ME ON SUNDAYS.

By the following week their windows had become a small appointment. Daniel began keeping a spare pad on the sill. He bought better coffee. When Ruth missed a night, he stayed at his desk until six, worrying that he had mistaken a brief acquaintance for something he was entitled to keep.

The question on the glass

She returned the next evening with a new book and an apology written across two sheets. Daniel asked whether she was all right. Ruth nodded, then frowned past him. She pointed at the wall behind his chair. He turned to look at his bookshelf, the narrow hallway, his kitchen door.

When he turned back, she was holding a question: WHERE IS YOUR DOOR? Daniel circled his finger toward the hallway. Ruth looked more troubled. She drew a rectangle containing a table and a chair, surrounded on all four sides by a wall. A little figure sat at the table. Above it she had written his name.

He stood and walked into the hallway. From the kitchen he heard three sharp knocks. Ruth was tapping her window with her knuckles. When Daniel returned, she was waving both hands for him to stop. The next page said: YOU DID NOT GET UP.

Daniel looked behind his chair. A second chair stood between him and the hall, identical to the one he had left. On it lay his jacket, arranged as though someone had just slipped out of it. He touched the collar. It was warm. His own chair, the one by the window, now had the jacket too.

He called his landlord. The phone rang inside the wall beside the bookshelf. Daniel knew that particular ringtone; the landlord used an old song with a trumpet introduction. When he ended the call, the music stopped. Across the courtyard Ruth pressed her whole palm against the glass. DO NOT SIT DOWN, her next page said.

Someone at the table

He tried the hallway again. This time it ended after two steps in a wall painted the same gray as his bedroom. He knocked, hard enough to hurt. A patient knock answered from the other side, one beat after his own. The apartment smelled of fresh paint and warm printer paper.

At the window Ruth held up a larger drawing. It showed the courtyard, her room, and Daniel's room opposite. Every other window was crossed out. Beside Daniel she had drawn a seated figure in heavy pencil. She pointed to his chair, then covered her eyes.

He understood enough to stop looking at the desk. Whatever sat there was becoming easier to see. A hand rested on the invoice beneath his first message. Its fingers had the old yellow nicotine stains Daniel's father had carried, though Daniel had never smoked. He stared at Ruth's window instead. 'What do I do?' he shouted.

She cupped a hand to her ear. The courtyard took his words and returned them in a dry flutter, like pages turning. Ruth wrote: TELL ME SOMETHING I DO NOT KNOW. Daniel shouted his full name. She shook her head and pointed behind him. From the chair, someone recited it in Daniel's voice.

He thought of work, his school, the addresses where he had lived. All were written somewhere, filed in envelopes or stored in a machine. Then he remembered his sister learning to swim. For three summers she had refused to enter a pool unless he called the water soup. He had not thought about it in years.

'My sister thought the swimming pool was soup,' he shouted. The voice behind him stumbled over the word. Ruth leaned forward. Daniel told her about his sister's orange armbands, their father's terrible sandwiches, the cold tile where they waited for her courage. By the time he reached the sandwiches, Ruth was laughing.

A place to knock

In the hallway, something clicked. Daniel kept talking. He told Ruth about the first sentence he had ever been paid to translate, and the shame of buying flowers for himself and claiming they were a gift. Behind him, the other voice grew quieter. It could repeat his facts. It could not quite tell his stories before he did.

Ruth disappeared from her window. Daniel almost turned toward the chair. Then he heard running footsteps above the courtyard, followed by a door slamming and a knock in his hallway. Three knocks. He walked toward them without looking back. The wall at the end of the hall now held his front door.

He opened it on a woman in slippers, carrying a pad thick with taped-together pages. She was much shorter than he had imagined. 'You really do need better coffee,' she said. Daniel stepped outside. Behind him, his apartment light went out. They stood together while someone on the other side carefully turned the key.

The landlord found nothing wrong the next morning. One chair, one jacket, a perfectly ordinary hallway. Daniel moved his desk into the living room, where visitors could see it from the door. He stopped working through every night. Ruth gave him a book she had repaired, its spine stronger than the original.

They still live across the courtyard. On Sundays Daniel sometimes carries her groceries upstairs. They do not leave notes at the windows anymore. Once, while Ruth was away visiting her son, the yellow square lit up at three in the morning. A sheet of white paper appeared against the glass. Daniel closed his curtains before it could turn around.
