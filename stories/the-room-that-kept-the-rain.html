The Room That Kept the Rain

During a summer drought, a hotel cleaner finds rain falling inside an unused room. The water is rising toward the ceiling, and something is waiting above it.

The dry season

By the thirty-eighth day without rain, the hotel had stopped watering its window boxes. Nadia carried drinking water to work because the taps ran brown each morning. At seven, while she was folding sheets on the second-floor landing, she heard a downpour behind the door of Room 209.

The room had been out of use since she joined the hotel. Its lock took an old metal key instead of a card. The owner said the bathroom needed work and that she should leave it alone. Now a thin line of water showed beneath the door, darkening the carpet without spreading any farther.

Nadia knocked. No answer. She fetched a bucket, used the key, and pushed the door inward. Rain fell through the room in fine silver threads. It came from the carpet and rose toward the ceiling. The bed stood beneath it, its blankets lifting and settling as though someone had just climbed out.

She stayed at the threshold. The air smelled of wet leaves, quite different from the hot tarmac outside. A drop struck the underside of her chin. It traveled upward over her cheek and vanished into her hair. On the ceiling, water gathered in a dark, rippling pool.

Nadia closed the door and called the owner. He let her explain without interrupting. Then he asked whether she had moved anything. When she said no, he told her to lock the room and wait in reception. He was not coming to the hotel. He was leaving town.

The old photograph

Nadia did not wait in reception. Seven guests were eating breakfast, and one had already complained that the ceiling above his shower felt damp. She asked the porter, Luis, to help her inspect the top floor. He was older than the owner and had a habit of carrying keys to doors that no longer existed.

At the mention of 209 he put down his coffee. He showed her a photograph in the back office: the hotel beside a river, a low iron bridge visible through the ground-floor windows. Nadia knew the river. It ran behind the hotel, deep in a concrete channel. The bridge in the photograph had been removed long before she was born.

A storm had flooded the ground floor in 1963, Luis said. The owner at the time had paid to have every damaged room stripped and replastered. Except one. In 209, above the flood line, a guest had woken to find river water across the ceiling. Her traveling case was still there when they opened the room. She was not.

'It happens when the river gets low,' Luis said. He had seen it twice before. Both times the former owner had waited for rain, and both times the room had dried by morning. The present owner had sold the old contents online in April. Nadia looked at the photograph again. A brass disk hung beside the reception bell. She had seen its pale outline on the wall.

Luis opened the old inventory. The missing disk was a flood marker salvaged from the bridge. Its description included a warning scratched into its reverse: KEEP ABOVE WATER. In the auction photograph, the buyer had displayed it face down beside a ruler. Behind its reflected edge, Nadia could make out part of a wet hand.

The high-water mark

By ten, water had reached the ceiling outside 209. It hung in a long shallow ribbon above the hallway, reflecting the carpet beneath it. Nadia looked up and saw a lamp swinging below an iron bridge. The view had depth. Somewhere beyond the plaster, a current was passing in the dark.

She asked the guests to leave and called the fire service. The man on the phone asked where the water was entering. Nadia tried to explain. While she spoke, a suitcase rose slowly from the carpet, turned on its handle, and slid into the ceiling without leaving a hole. She stopped explaining and said the building was unsafe.

Luis had found the buyer's address. A dealer fifteen minutes away had put the marker in his shop window. Nadia drove there in her work clothes. The dealer was standing on the pavement, staring at the glass. Behind it, old clocks and porcelain plates hung at different heights in a room filling from the top down.

She broke a small corner of the shop window with the tire iron from her car. Water spilled inward through the gap, against the heat of the pavement. The marker lay on the sill, its brass face perfectly dry. Nadia hooked it with the tire iron and pulled it outside. Every object in the shop fell at once.

On the back, beneath the scratched warning, the brass had been rubbed smooth by many hands. Nadia wrapped it in a towel. The towel grew heavy and cold. From inside it came the slow clanking sound of chains beneath a bridge. She drove with both windows open and kept her eyes on the road.

Above the water

Luis was waiting outside the hotel. The emergency crew had cleared the building and were checking the river wall. Nadia showed a firefighter the old photograph and the disk. He was looking at it when a chair passed the second-floor window, drifting upward with all four legs pointing toward the sky.

Nadia and Luis went in with two firefighters. The old hook beside the reception bell was still dry, but water trembled along the ceiling, lower than before. Through its surface Nadia saw ironwork and a pale, upturned face. The face moved with them as they crossed the lobby. It was not a reflection.

She could not lift the disk without standing on a chair. Luis held it steady while the firefighter passed her the towel. As she raised her arms, the cold surface above her bent downward. Fingers pressed against it from the other side. They spread carefully, testing where her head would be.

Nadia found the hook by touch. The disk's loop slipped off once. Water closed over the crown of her head, and for a moment she heard a river hammering against stone. She pushed upward again. The loop caught. Luis pulled her down so hard that the chair overturned beneath her.

The water withdrew without falling. It streamed up the walls toward 209, carrying the sound of loose chains. In the sudden quiet, they heard a single suitcase wheel rolling across the floor above them. The firefighter helped Nadia outside. Her hair was dry. One of her earrings was gone.

The hotel never reopened. The disk remains high on the reception wall, checked each month by someone from the town. Nadia works in a supermarket now. When rain finally came that summer, she stood beneath the loading-bay roof and watched it fall in the proper direction. In the gutter at her feet, something small and silver turned once. She let the water carry it away.
