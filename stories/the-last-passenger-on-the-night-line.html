The Last Passenger on the Night Line

On her final shift, a train conductor finds a passenger holding a ticket for a station closed forty years ago. He has been trying to get off for a very long time.

One more ticket

Mira had a retirement card in her bag and nine minutes left on the last train when she found the man in the rear carriage. He wore a dark suit with a paper flower in its buttonhole. Beside him sat a small cake box, held level on a folded coat. He offered his ticket before she asked.

It was a stiff rectangle of yellow card, the kind Mira had used as a child. A neat round hole had been punched through the fare. The destination was Vale End. She read it twice. Vale End had closed forty years earlier, when a landslide destroyed the little station and the line was diverted through the hill.

'I'm afraid we don't stop there,' she said. The man nodded as though she had told him something about the weather. 'I know. I keep hoping they'll put it back.' He looked at the cake box. 'It's getting rather late.'

Mira asked where he actually needed to go. He gave an address in a village the new route no longer served. She could arrange a taxi from the terminus, she said. The man thanked her, but when the train entered the tunnel his face became difficult to see. The paper flower remained clear against his lapel.

She looked toward the connecting door. There were three more passengers in the next carriage. They sat very straight, each holding an old yellow ticket. Mira had checked that carriage at the previous stop. It had been empty.

The old route

She called the driver on the internal radio. Rafi said the instruments were normal, but the tunnel was taking longer than it should. Mira told him about the tickets. He did not laugh. Instead, he asked whether the passengers had bags. She looked through the connecting door. A shopping basket, a child's satchel, a violin case. 'Yes,' she said. Rafi swore softly.

His first instructor had told him a story about the old line. During the last evening service before the landslide, a signal failure had stopped the train short of Vale End. The passengers walked back along the track toward the station. There had been heavy rain. Some never reached it. Their belongings were recovered in the cutting.

Mira remembered the incident. Her father had worked on the recovery team, and for weeks afterward he had come home carrying his boots in a plastic bag. He never told her what he had found. Rafi said they should keep moving. As long as the train stayed on the new line, the tunnel had to end.

The man in the suit was listening. 'It does end,' he said. 'That's the trouble.' He opened the cake box. Inside was a small cake with white icing, its careful lettering blurred by condensation: WELCOME HOME, JUNE. A narrow slice had been cut from it. The cut surface was fresh.

'My wife came home from the hospital that afternoon,' he said. 'Nothing dramatic. A new hip. I thought she'd be tired of their puddings.' He smiled, embarrassed by his own explanation. Then the train lights dimmed. Across the aisle, the dark window showed a platform with a wooden bench and a white-painted sign: VALE END.

The announcement

Mira put her hand against the window. They were still moving. She could feel the wheels beneath her feet. But the platform stayed level with the carriage, its bench and station sign holding perfectly still. Beyond it, people waited beneath umbrellas. None looked toward the train.

The man stood. So did the passengers in the next carriage. Along the train, connecting doors opened in succession. Mira saw more yellow tickets, more faces reflected in glass. The radio hissed. Rafi said the controls were not responding. The speedometer still read fifty, but the platform had appeared outside his cab too.

The man in the suit pressed the door button. Nothing happened. He pressed again. His hand began to shake. 'Every time,' he said. The woman with the shopping basket reached past him, then withdrew her hand. From the other carriages came a quiet, patient sound of people trying handles.

Mira looked at her watch. The second hand was moving backward. In the window beside her face was the reflection of a girl in a school uniform, clutching a lunchbox. Mira had carried that lunchbox the year her father stopped working on the railway. She took her hand away from the glass. The girl did not.

She asked the man whether anyone had ever told them they had arrived. He looked puzzled. In forty years, perhaps everyone had done what she first did: explained that the station was closed. A conductor knew the difference between a place existing and a journey being allowed to end. Mira reached for the public-address handset.

'We are now arriving at Vale End,' she said. Her voice sounded small in the long train. 'This service terminates here. Please take all your belongings with you.' The man closed his cake box. The platform lamps came on, one after another.

End of service

The doors opened. Cold air moved through the carriage, carrying wet earth and the sharp smell of creosote. The passengers stepped down without hurrying. Mira counted them because counting was something she could still do. Twenty-three. The last was the man with the cake. He turned and offered her the small paper flower.

She almost took it. Then she saw that the girl in the window had moved into the aisle. She stood exactly where Mira stood, reaching for the flower with a much smaller hand. On the platform, beneath one of the umbrellas, Mira recognized her father. He looked as he had before his illness. He had been dead for nine years.

Rafi spoke in her ear, very clearly. 'Mira. Stay with the train.' She lowered her hand. The girl took one step toward the open door and vanished. On the platform, her father nodded once, as though she had answered a question correctly. Mira told the man with the cake to keep his flower.

She checked the carriage one last time. The seats were empty. The old yellow ticket lay on the floor, its round hole dark against the vinyl. She picked it up and held it through the doorway. The man took it with two fingers. Then Mira pressed the close button and waited until every light above the doors had gone out.

The tunnel ended. Rafi brought the train into the terminus three minutes late. No one on the platform noticed anything unusual about the empty carriages or the two railway workers standing together beside the cab. Mira called home before collecting her retirement cake from the staff room.

In the morning she found something in her bag beneath the card. It was her old lunchbox, clean and empty. On its lid, her father had written her name in the black marker he used for his tools. Mira washed it, dried it carefully, and began taking it on walks. When someone asked where she was going, she found she enjoyed being able to choose.
