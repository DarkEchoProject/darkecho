The Voice Left on Side B

A sound archivist restores an anonymous cassette. Its missing voice begins borrowing the small sounds from her flat, one recording at a time.

Room tone

Ada made her living rescuing voices from damaged tape. She had cleaned wedding speeches, interviews with retired miners, and one entire hour of a man attempting to teach his parrot a telephone number. She knew the difference between silence and a recording of an empty room. The anonymous cassette contained neither.

It arrived in a padded envelope with no return address. A handwritten label read HOUSE RECORDING, SIDE A. Beneath that, in smaller letters: Leave the other side alone. Ada photographed the label, logged the donation, and listened to Side A through her studio headphones.

There was a kettle, a chair moving, somebody opening a window. After nine minutes a woman's voice said, 'That's everything, I think.' The window shut. The tape kept turning, carrying a soft hiss that changed whenever Ada moved her head. It felt oddly like listening to a room through a keyhole.

She turned the cassette over to check whether Side B had been erased. The first sound was her own kettle clicking off. Not a similar kettle. Hers had a loose lid that rattled twice before the switch released. On the tape, it rattled twice. From her kitchen came a bright mechanical click, without its usual rattle.

Ada took off her headphones and put the kettle on again. Steam lifted the lid. It trembled visibly. She heard nothing until the switch clicked. On the recording, the missing rattle played once more, followed by the scrape of a chair.

The inventory

She spent half an hour finding explanations. Fatigue. A blocked ear. An electrical fault in the kettle. Then she pulled out her kitchen chair. Its rubber foot caught on the floor in the usual short stutter, but the movement was silent. In the headphones, somebody dragged a chair across a room.

She stopped the deck. The flat settled around her. The refrigerator hummed, traffic passed, a neighbor laughed. She could hear perfectly well. On her desk, the cassette counter stood at 0037. Her digital capture showed thirty-seven seconds of sound, ending with the click of her own mouse.

Ada clicked the mouse. The button moved beneath her finger without making a noise. She played the capture through the computer speakers. Kettle, chair, mouse. After each sound came a short inhalation. Someone was listening back. She had restored hundreds of breaths, softened their harshness, removed them when clients asked. This one seemed to be getting easier.

She searched the archive's accession book for the envelope's postmark. There was an older donation from the same town: household recordings, fifteen cassettes, withdrawn by the donor in 1988. A note clipped to the record said the donor had requested that every copy be destroyed. The archivist had written, No copies made. Underneath, in a different hand: Ask what he means by copies.

Ada called the number on the old form. It had been disconnected. She called her colleague Martin instead and described the problem badly enough that he asked whether she had eaten. She tried to laugh. Her throat moved. Over the phone Martin said, 'Ada? Are you still there?'

A voice assembled

She touched her throat. 'Yes,' she said. The word came out normally. Relief made her laugh again, and the laugh made no sound. On the computer, the digital capture had grown longer even though she had stopped recording. At its end, a woman laughed with Ada's small catch between breaths.

The capture was acquiring the flat's sounds in the order they had been taken. After the laugh came footsteps, the zipper of her jacket, a cough. Ada watched the waveform extend. She unplugged the computer. The speakers gave one final soft breath as their power light went dark.

The cassette itself was warm. Against the window she could see the strip of tape inside the shell, a brown ribbon wound around its little white wheels. The label on Side A had lifted at one corner. Under it were words pressed so hard into the paper that they had marked the plastic: It cannot finish a voice you do not use.

Ada understood why the original donor had asked about copies. Her capture had not reproduced the sound. It had made another place for the listener to wait. She thought of cloud backups, automatic uploads, the archive's preservation server. The network cable lay disconnected beside her laptop. She worked from home because the archive could not afford to heat her studio every evening. For once, the economy had helped.

She wrote Martin a message asking him to come, then stopped before sending it. The keyboard had fallen silent beneath her fingers. On the dark computer's speakers, keys began tapping. A voice was practicing her name, using her laugh for the first syllable.

The last sound

Ada worked with the headphones off. She erased the local capture, removed the laptop's battery, and pulled the ribbon from the cassette with a pencil. Every few feet, a room sound returned: the mouse click, the zipper, her chair scraping across the floor. The tape came out in tangled loops. In the kitchen, the kettle began rattling though it was cold.

At the very end of the ribbon was a clear leader with no magnetic coating. Knotted around it was a short length of older brown tape. Ada held it up to the desk lamp. The knot had been tied by hand. Someone else had reached this point and decided that a warning was enough.

From the unplugged speakers came her mother's voice. It asked Ada to wait. Ada's mother was alive, away on a walking holiday, and had never been recorded in this flat. The thing had learned what a plea should sound like. Ada cut through the knot and the clear leader together.

The refrigerator stopped. So did the traffic and the neighbor's television. For a few seconds she stood in a silence so complete that she felt her heartbeat in her teeth. Then a truck passed below the window. Her kettle's lid clicked as the metal cooled. Ada laughed once, shakily, and heard herself.

Martin arrived twenty minutes later. He found her sorting the ruined tape into a metal biscuit tin. He asked whether she could play him what she had heard. Ada said no, and he accepted that with less explanation than she expected. They drank coffee in the kitchen. She could hear his spoon against the china. He left his phone switched off on the table.

She did not send the tin anywhere. She kept it in the archive's locked cupboard, with an honest description and no listening copy. Sometimes a new colleague asked why its catalog entry had no audio sample. Ada always answered in person, away from microphones, and made sure they heard every word.
