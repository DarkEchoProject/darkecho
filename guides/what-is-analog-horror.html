Formats and storytelling

What Is Analog Horror? A Guide to the Uneasy Signal

Old broadcasts, institutional voices, and images that seem to have survived the wrong recording. Learn how to read the format without confusing its fiction with evidence.

By Dark Echo EditorialPublished Nov 25, 2026About 3 min read

The short answer

Analog horror uses the appearance and conventions of older recorded media to tell unsettling stories. The aged presentation is a creative device; it is not proof that the events or recording history are real.

Start with the form, not a claim of authenticity

For this guide, think of analog horror as horror that borrows the visual and narrative language of older media. A television announcement, an instructional tape, or an apparently recovered recording can supply a frame for the story. The audience is invited to meet it as a piece of media with a supposed history.

This working definition is useful because it leaves room for different kinds of stories. The important question is what the presentation asks you to believe or expect. A work can evoke older recording equipment without having been made on that equipment. In his production note for Local 58's Contingency, Kris Straub explains making the aged graphics and effects deliberately.

Why a familiar voice can become unsettling

A useful way to read the format is to ask who seems to be speaking. An announcer, a station ident, or an official-looking message can make an instruction feel routine before its content becomes strange. Notice the moment when the form remains calm but the message no longer fits the situation you expected.

That is an interpretive tool, not a rule that every work must follow. In a direct interview, Straub discusses the uncertainty of encountering television without complete context. You can use that idea as a viewing question: what would this fragment feel like if you did not know who selected it or what came before it?

Distortion is a storytelling choice

Try describing the effect before explaining it. Is text difficult to read? Does a voice appear separated from its source? Does a break interrupt information you wanted? These observations are more useful than immediately calling every imperfection a hidden code.

You can then test a reading. Perhaps the interruption withholds something important, or perhaps the familiar format makes an impossible message more uncomfortable. Keep the interpretation beside the observation that prompted it. When you cannot connect them clearly, leave the explanation provisional rather than adding more lore to make it fit.

Analog horror, found footage, and ARGs

These labels ask different questions. Analog horror describes a kind of presentation. Found footage describes the fiction that a recording has been discovered or recovered. An alternate reality game, or ARG, concerns a participatory clue trail. One work may use more than one approach, but a grainy image alone does not establish all three.

For example, our Backrooms file starts with a particular upload and its dates, while the This House Has People in It file examines a surveillance presentation and its wider fictional context. Read each file on its own terms. Calling both unsettling is reasonable; assuming they share a universe or one explanation would need separate support.

A small viewing notebook

Use three short columns: the detail, your interpretation, and the source you would need to check it. A date printed within a story belongs in the first column. A theory about when the video was actually made belongs in the second until you have a publication or production record.

On a second viewing, look for something that might challenge your first explanation. If your reading can accommodate every possible detail without changing, ask what would make it wrong. You do not need to decode every frame to have a satisfying encounter with the work. A precise question is often a better ending than a confident but unsupported answer.
