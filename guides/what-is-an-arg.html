What Is an ARG? A Guide to Fictional Clue Trails

Understand alternate reality games, keep a useful clue log, and separate a story-world document from evidence about the world outside it.

By Dark Echo EditorialPublished Nov 25, 2026About 3 min read

The short answer

An alternate reality game uses a story and clues that participants investigate, often across different media. It can ask an audience to help make progress; it is not the same thing as augmented reality or simply an unexplained video.

An investigation designed as a story

ARG stands for alternate reality game. It is a form of participatory storytelling that can spread clues and narrative material across websites, recordings, messages and other media. Participants do some of the connecting: finding an entry point, comparing records and following a designed trail. The IGDA ARG SIG's 2006 white paper is a historical introduction to these practices.

The initials are easy to confuse with augmented reality. That is a different idea. An ARG does not need an overlay on a phone camera to give its audience an investigation. Nor does every mysterious upload become an ARG just because people gather to discuss it. Look for the specific invitation to participate and the material the project actually supplies.

What counts as an entry point?

An entry point is the piece through which you first meet a project: perhaps a video, a public page, or a document linked from the creator's own material. Start by recording that address and the date you encountered it. This gives you a route back when later discoveries make the original context harder to remember.

Keep the publisher and the fictional speaker separate. A page can be presented as a station archive or company report while being part of a creator's project. For instance, Straub's own project listing identifies Local58TV directly. A creator-side record helps you locate the work without treating every claim made inside its story as a real institutional statement.

A clue log that another person can follow

For each step, record what you found, where it appeared and exactly how it led onward. If the step involves changing text, write down the method so another participant can repeat it. Keep a screenshot or a short description for your own notes, but retain the link and context as well. A cropped image can lose the details needed to retrace a step.

Then add a separate theory column. A connection between two names might be a productive hypothesis, while a link supplied by the project is a different kind of record. Keeping both visible lets the group discuss interpretations without turning the most popular suggestion into an established fact.

Stay within the project’s invitation

Use the material the identified project makes available. A mystery is not a reason to access someone else's account, expose private details, or involve an unrelated person. When a trail seems to point outside the project, pause and ask what connects that destination to the work. A similar name or visual resemblance is not a sufficient bridge.

You can also stop without completing the trail. An unsolved clue does not create an obligation to contact a stranger or spend money. These are practical boundaries for a satisfying hobby: the investigation should remain something you choose, with evidence that can be discussed openly among participants.

A puzzle, a theory, and a record

Our 11B-X-1371 file is a useful place to practise separating a production record from proposed meanings. The This House Has People in It file offers another question: what belongs to the short and what belongs to its companion fiction? Neither exercise requires declaring that every unsettling work uses the same game structure.

Before presenting a solution, ask whether you can show each step, whether someone else can reproduce it, and which parts depend on interpretation. A clear account of an unfinished investigation can be more useful than a complete-looking answer built from guesses. The goal is to make the trail legible, not to make uncertainty disappear.

Try this on your next video

Choose one clearly identified fictional project. Record its entry point, one clue and the step connecting it to the next page. Mark anything you cannot trace as a theory rather than silently adding it to the story.
